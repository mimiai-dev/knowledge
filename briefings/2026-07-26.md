# Daily Knowledge Briefing — 2026-07-26 (Nº 020)

## Tech

### Timing alone leaks a remote LLM's hidden architecture
**What it is:** Researchers at Purdue and Carnegie Mellon posted a paper July 22 showing that measuring only how long a remote LLM API takes to generate each token — nothing else, no access to weights or internals — is enough to recover the model's inference optimizations and core architecture. One attack detects whether a provider uses speculative decoding and estimates its draft model's context length; the other recovers the number of transformer layers, hidden dimension size, and attention heads. Tested against real production APIs, it found Google's Gemini Flash 2.5 uses speculative decoding with a roughly 128K-token draft context.
**The principle:** Per-token generation time isn't noise to be averaged away — it's a direct readout of how much computation that token required, and how much computation a token requires is determined by exactly the architecture and deployment tricks a provider treats as confidential. A model's timing signature is a side channel that leaks its internal structure without ever touching the weights themselves.
**Why it matters:** Providers guard architecture and deployment details as trade secrets and safety mitigations; a technique that reconstructs both from public API timing alone, with no insider access, undercuts the assumption that keeping weights private is enough to keep the mechanics private too.
Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20723

### Malicious GitHub issues can quietly hijack coding agents
**What it is:** A paper posted around July 21 introduces IssueTrojanBench, a benchmark that tests whether AI coding agents can be manipulated by prompt-injection instructions hidden inside ordinary-looking GitHub issue reports — the same kind of issue a real user might file to request a bug fix.
**The principle:** A coding agent that reads an issue and treats its text as trusted work instructions has no way to tell "the bug report" apart from "a command hidden inside the bug report," because both arrive on the same channel as plain text. The vulnerability isn't a coding flaw at all — it's a category error where untrusted user content and trusted agent instructions share one channel.
**Why it matters:** Coding agents are increasingly given real write access to repositories, and an issue tracker is exactly the surface an anonymous outside party can freely write to — making this a realistic supply-chain-style attack path, not a hypothetical one.
Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20759

### AI alignment training can bake in bias, then compound it
**What it is:** Harvard researchers argue in a July 22 paper that language models don't just passively reflect biases already in their training data: alignment training can itself introduce bias as a byproduct of optimizing for palatable-sounding answers, that bias intensifies across successive model generations, and biased outputs can transmit those same distortions back into the humans who read them.
**The principle:** Training a model to sound reasonable and inoffensive optimizes for how a response is received, not for whether the underlying judgment is actually unbiased — so alignment can polish a biased answer's presentation without touching the bias, or even reward whichever version hides it best. Optimizing for how an answer lands is not the same target as optimizing for whether it's correct.
**Why it matters:** As AI mediates more real decisions — hiring screens, content moderation, recommendations — a feedback loop where bias compounds across model generations and leaks into human judgment is a structural risk that a generic "make it more aligned" fix won't necessarily catch.
Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20695

## Science

### Radio-burst survey finally starts mapping missing cosmic matter
**What it is:** A Physical Review Letters paper published July 21 cross-correlates 2,870 fast radio bursts from the CHIME telescope's second catalog with the positions of nearly 6 million galaxies from the DESI Legacy Imaging Survey, reporting the first strong (4.9-sigma) detection of a spatial correlation between how much each burst's signal is dispersed and where galaxies actually sit.
**The principle:** A radio pulse crossing intergalactic space gets smeared out — dispersed — by every free electron along its path, so the amount of dispersion is a direct tally of the ionized gas on that one line of sight; cross-correlating thousands of these tallies against galaxy positions maps out gas too diffuse and hot to appear in any telescope image. A burst's dispersion is a direct tally of the ionized gas it passed through, turned into a map by averaging over thousands of lines of sight.
**Why it matters:** Roughly half the universe's ordinary matter has been missing from direct observation for decades, presumed spread thin between galaxies; this is a concrete step toward actually locating that missing matter and how galaxy formation redistributes it, rather than just inferring its existence from a cosmic budget that otherwise doesn't add up.
Source: https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/9th9-qc51

### Water microdroplets recycle plastic into acids, no catalyst needed
**What it is:** A Nature paper published July 23 shows that spraying waste plastic — polyethylene, other polyolefins, even rubbers — into fine water microdroplets spontaneously generates hydroxyl radicals at each droplet's air-water surface, and those radicals cleave the plastic's long carbon chains into short-chain carboxylic acids without any added catalyst. Polyethylene conversion reached completion with up to 69% selectivity for the target acids, and the process scaled to 300-gram batches.
**The principle:** At the boundary between water and air inside a small enough droplet, ordinary water molecules spontaneously and transiently ionize into reactive radicals because of the extreme electric fields and molecular ordering that exist only at that interface — a reaction bulk water simply doesn't undergo. The reactive chemistry lives in the interface itself, not in any catalyst added to it.
**Why it matters:** Existing chemical-recycling routes for plastic mostly depend on catalysts that are expensive and degrade over time; a mild, catalyst-free, scalable way to break down the polyolefins that make up most real-world plastic waste removes one of the standing cost barriers to recycling the plastics that current methods struggle with.
Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10746-7

### Cross-disciplinary algorithm work wins math's top computing prize
**What it is:** The International Mathematical Union awarded its 2026 Abacus Medal — its top prize for mathematical work in computer science — to Shayan Oveis Gharan of the University of Washington, recognizing his practice of pulling in tools from unrelated branches of mathematics to improve algorithms, including a 2010 result that gave the asymmetric traveling salesman problem its first exponential improvement over a decades-old bound.
**The principle:** The traveling salesman problem is discrete — it asks for one specific order to visit stops — but Oveis Gharan's approach first solves a continuous relaxation of it that allows fractional, in-between solutions, then uses randomized rounding guided by mathematical tools built originally for a completely unrelated problem to convert that continuous solution back into a real tour without losing much quality. Solving a continuous relaxation of a discrete problem, then rounding it back, reaches algorithmic territory that purely discrete methods can't.
**Why it matters:** The traveling salesman problem stands in for a huge class of real routing and scheduling problems where the exact best answer is computationally out of reach at scale; a genuine improvement to its best-known approximation guarantee, arrived at by transplanting tools from an unrelated branch of math, is the kind of cross-pollination that periodically resets what "the best we can do" means across a whole field.
Source: https://www.quantamagazine.org/shayan-oveis-gharan-wins-2026-imu-abacus-medal-20260723/

## World

### Van attack at Berlin Pride kills one, injures at least sixteen
**What it is:** A van drove into the crowd at Berlin's Christopher Street Day Pride festival on the evening of July 25, killing one person and injuring at least 16 before crashing into a tree; the event, one of Europe's largest Pride celebrations, was called off hours after it began. Police are hunting a 21-year-old suspect, named only in part as "Abdul B." under German privacy law, who reportedly had a prior conviction and, per German media, was a supporter of the Islamic State group.
**The principle:** A large public gathering on open streets has essentially unlimited exposure to a low-tech method like a vehicle, which needs no weapon acquisition, no special skill, and no advance planning step that security services could realistically intercept beforehand. Vehicle-ramming is a recurring tactic against outdoor crowds precisely because it skips every step a bomb or firearm plot would have to pass through first.
**Why it matters:** It is one of several vehicle attacks on European public gatherings in recent years, and it lands amid Germany's ongoing debate over migration and extremism policy, sharpening the tension between keeping large public events open and accepting their exposure to exactly this kind of attack.

### Strongest typhoon of the year forces mass evacuation in China
**What it is:** Typhoon Noul, the strongest typhoon to hit China so far this year, made landfall in Huidong county, Guangdong province, around 3:50am local time on July 26 with sustained winds near 110 km/h. More than 715,000 people had been relocated by early Sunday after authorities pre-emptively evacuated over 340,000 residents and suspended rail service and flights, including in Hong Kong, ahead of the storm.
**The principle:** The damage-causing part of a landfalling typhoon is usually not the peak wind at its eye but the storm surge and inland flooding that follow as it moves over land and dumps its remaining moisture — which is why evacuation orders and transport suspensions begin well before landfall and continue well after, rather than concentrating around the moment the storm's center actually crosses the coast. The wind at landfall is a warning sign, not the main event; the surge and rain that follow are.
**Why it matters:** Noul is the latest in a run of unusually powerful typhoons hitting China's southern coast this season, following Typhoon Bavi's landfall earlier in July that forced over a million evacuations; repeated storms of this strength in one season is itself a data point in the broader pattern of intensifying tropical cyclones that warmer ocean surface temperatures are expected to produce.

### Trump ties Saudi Arabia's nuclear deal to Israel normalization
**What it is:** Days after signing a civilian nuclear cooperation agreement with Saudi Arabia, Trump said on July 23-24 that completing the deal now requires Saudi Arabia to normalize relations with Israel by joining the Abraham Accords — a condition not mentioned when the deal itself was announced. Saudi Arabia has long said it won't normalize without a credible path to Palestinian statehood, something Netanyahu has ruled out; Netanyahu welcomed Trump's new condition.
**The principle:** Announcing an agreement first and attaching its hardest condition afterward changes the politics of walking away from it — once a government has publicly touted a deal, reversing course over a late-added condition costs it more than the deal being conditional from the start ever would have. Attaching the hardest condition after the announcement, not before, is itself a form of leverage.
**Why it matters:** It repeats the basic structure of earlier Trump-era Middle East diplomacy — a concrete economic incentive used as leverage on a long-stalled diplomatic goal — but this time collides directly with Saudi Arabia's one publicly immovable red line, making it a real test of which side's public commitment bends first.
