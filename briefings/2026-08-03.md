## Tech

### GitHub's code-review AI got worse when its tools got better
**What it is:** GitHub swapped Copilot code review's bespoke tools for the same shared grep, glob, and view tools that power the Copilot CLI, expecting a clean upgrade. Instead, benchmarks showed reviews got more expensive and caught fewer real issues. The fix wasn't better tools — it was rewriting the agent's instructions to match how a human actually reads a pull request: start from the diff, form specific questions, glob when the path is uncertain, grep for candidates, batch cheap discovery before opening files, and only call view once you know exactly what you need. That single change flipped the regression into roughly 20% lower average review cost with no drop in quality.

**The principle:** The new tools were more capable in general, but they were tuned for open-ended exploration of an unfamiliar codebase, not for verifying specific claims against a diff already in hand — a different task calls for a different search order, and nobody had rewritten the workflow to match. A more capable tool without a workflow rebuilt around how the task is actually done can make an agent worse, not better.

**Why it matters:** It's a clean case study in agent engineering: the intuitive fix — give the agent better tools — made things worse, and the real fix was matching the workflow to the tools, a lesson that generalizes to any agentic system built from shared primitives.

Source: https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/better-tools-made-copilot-code-review-worse-heres-how-we-actually-improved-it/

### A shared toolkit standardizes how researchers find AI "circuits"
**What it is:** Researchers released CircuitKIT, an open toolkit for mechanistic interpretability — the project of reverse-engineering which small groups of neurons or attention heads inside a neural network are responsible for a specific behavior, known as a "circuit." It provides a typed, serializable representation of a circuit plus a suite of discovery algorithms and diagnostic tools, letting different labs' circuit-finding methods be compared and reused instead of each group maintaining its own one-off scripts.

**The principle:** Interpretability findings have piled up as real scientific results built on non-reusable code, because every lab writes its own bespoke tooling to find and check circuits — nothing forces two labs' "circuit" to mean the same data structure. Standardizing what a circuit is represented as, not just how to find one, is what makes interpretability findings comparable across labs instead of one-off scripts.

**Why it matters:** Interpretability is one of the few tools that can check what a model is actually doing rather than what it claims to do — but the field's progress has been bottlenecked by fragmented tooling, and shared infrastructure like this is what lets results actually accumulate.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19317

### A new benchmark tests whether AI can be talked into things, not just told
**What it is:** A new paper introduces "adversarial pragmatics," a benchmark and annotation protocol for testing how language models handle instructions that are technically ambiguous but exploit the same tricks people use to manipulate meaning: a command embedded inside a quotation, scope ambiguity ("the emails from Bob" — sent by Bob, or about him?), indirect speech acts, and multi-turn transcripts where an instruction from several turns back quietly still applies.

**The principle:** Most AI red-teaming tests direct, obviously malicious requests, but real manipulation — of people and of models — usually works through pragmatics, the layer of meaning that depends on context and inference rather than literal words, such as quoting a harmful instruction inside a story so it reads as fiction instead of a command. The exploit doesn't need information the model lacks — it needs an ambiguous reading the model resolves the wrong way, the same trick that works on people.

**Why it matters:** As models get more autonomy to act on multi-turn instructions, the attack surface shifts from "does it refuse an obvious bad request" to "can it be talked into one" — this benchmark targets exactly that harder, subtler failure mode.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01153

## Science

### Buried mammoths near Mexico City rewrite their family tree
**What it is:** Construction crews building a new airport north of Mexico City unearthed hundreds of mammoth skeletons trapped in an ancient lakebed. Researchers sequenced 61 complete mitochondrial genomes from the bones — more than doubling the world's total for Columbian mammoths — and, in a paper published in Science, found the animals form a previously unknown lineage that split from northern Columbian mammoths hundreds of thousands of years ago, genetically more distant from their US and Canadian relatives than expected.

**The principle:** A single well-preserved site with dozens of individuals lets researchers measure genetic variation within a population, not just compare one bone against another species — turning "here's a mammoth" into "here's how distinct this whole regional population actually was," which is only possible at scale. A find of dozens of individuals from one site turns paleogenomics into population genetics — measuring variation within a group, not just comparing isolated specimens.

**Why it matters:** Columbian mammoths were assumed to be one relatively uniform species ranging from Canada into the tropics; a genetically distinct tropical branch means the "mammoth" story looks more like several regional populations than one continuous species, reshaping how their extinction gets studied.

Source: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adt9651

### A thin film lets one weak voltage flip a strong, sideways charge
**What it is:** A paper in Nature demonstrates a layered ferroelectric material, Bi₄Ti₃O₁₂, in which the polarization that matters for devices — the direction its internal electric charge points — runs sideways through the film, normally the harder direction to control in a standard flat device. The researchers show that applying a small voltage straight through the film's thickness switches that sideways polarization anyway, because the two directions are coupled inside the material itself.

**The principle:** Most electronic devices apply voltage vertically, through a thin film's thickness — easy to build, but useless if the property you actually want to control points sideways. This material has an internal coupling, mediated by how its atomic layers tilt and rotate, that links vertical and horizontal polarization together, so switching one switches the other. Coupling two directions of polarization inside the material itself lets an easy-to-apply vertical voltage control a normally hard-to-reach sideways property.

**Why it matters:** Sideways ferroelectric polarization has long been attractive for certain memory and sensor designs but hard to integrate into standard chip geometries, which apply fields vertically; a material where the two are linked removes that mismatch and opens a more practical route to real devices.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10839-3

### A sea anemone gene reveals a second way immunity can evolve
**What it is:** Researchers studying the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis identified CARDIB, a gene sitting next to a cnidarian relative of the vertebrate immune system's virus-sensing genes. CARDIB closely resembles MAVS, the protein vertebrates use to switch on an antiviral response — but instead of turning immune genes on, CARDIB keeps them suppressed by default and is required for the anemone to properly ramp up defenses once a real virus appears.

**The principle:** Evolution can reuse the same molecular building block for an opposite job — a protein structurally similar to an "on switch" elsewhere in the animal kingdom is instead functioning here as an "off switch until needed," which means sequence similarity alone can't tell you the function; genomic context and direct experiment can. Structural resemblance to a known protein doesn't guarantee the same function — the same molecular shape has been repurposed from an "on" switch into a suppress-until-needed one.

**Why it matters:** Cnidarians split from our own lineage over 600 million years ago, so finding a MAVS-like antiviral system there, running in reverse from how it works in us, undercuts the assumption that this kind of immune signaling is one conserved system rather than one independently rewired more than once.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-026-03112-3

### The US and Japan jointly prop up the yen for the first time since 2011
**What it is:** On August 2–3, Japan's Ministry of Finance and the US Treasury confirmed a rare, coordinated intervention buying yen to halt its slide toward 40-year lows against the dollar — the first joint US-Japan currency intervention since the 2011 earthquake, and by some accounts the first fully coordinated support operation since 1998. Trump called it a "signal of friendship," and the yen jumped roughly 1.4% to a three-month high near 155 per dollar, extending a two-day rally.

**The principle:** A single country buying its own currency has limited firepower against the trillions traded daily in currency markets, so a joint intervention works less through the money actually spent and more as a credible signal that two major economies are aligned and prepared to keep acting — traders reprice expectations of future moves, not just react to the trade that already happened. A joint currency intervention works less through the money spent than through the signal that two governments are aligned and willing to keep intervening.

**Why it matters:** The last time these two countries coordinated like this was a crisis response to an earthquake; doing it now over ordinary currency weakness marks a notable shift in how seriously Washington and Tokyo are treating the yen's decline, with knock-on effects for global trade and capital flows.
