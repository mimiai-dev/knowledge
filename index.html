GitHub auto-holds suspicious Actions workflows before they run

What it is

GitHub Actions now automatically detects workflow runs it judges likely malicious — most often a pull request from an unfamiliar contributor that edits a workflow file to add credential-stealing steps — and holds them before any code executes. The run stays paused until a collaborator with write access reviews and explicitly approves it; nothing is silently blocked, it simply doesn't start. It currently covers public repositories on github.com only.

The principle

A CI workflow file runs with the same privileges as the repository's own automation, so a malicious edit is really a request to borrow that trust before anyone has reviewed it — the fix is making execution wait on review at the exact point untrusted code would otherwise start spending real credentials. Untrusted code shouldn't get to spend a repository's privileges before a human has looked at what it's asking to do.

Why it matters

Supply-chain attacks increasingly work by hijacking exactly this window — pushing a workflow edit that runs before review, then using the CI credentials it's handed to pivot further; catching that pattern automatically, by default, closes a gap that previously required every maintainer to notice on their own.

Averaging AI benchmark scores quietly inflates how much they prove

What it is

A position paper posted July 28 argues AI evaluation scores are treated as more durable and certain than they are. It proposes scoring evaluations as claims with three properties: how strong the evidence is (human judgment beats an automated metric), how narrow its scope is (a benchmark describes only the tested distribution), and how long it stays valid (scores decay as contamination accumulates and the world shifts).

The principle

When a model's evaluation blends several signals — automated metrics, LLM-as-judge ratings, human review, benchmark suites — by simple averaging, the combined confidence can end up higher than even the weakest signal deserves; a bad component doesn't just add noise, it gets diluted into an average that looks sturdier than any one part of it. Averaging several evaluation signals together can make a benchmark look more trustworthy than its weakest ingredient actually is.

Why it matters

As AI capability claims increasingly rest on stacked benchmark scores, a methodology that overstates confidence by construction means deployment and safety decisions get made on numbers that look sturdier than the evidence underneath them.

New architecture fixes how long-context AI models forget things

What it is

A paper posted July 28 introduces Raven, a linear-time sequence model that keeps a fixed set of memory slots and, at every step, decides which small subset to update — rather than touching all of memory like state-space models do, or only a nearby sliding window like windowed attention does.

The principle

Dense-update models refresh their entire memory on every token, so old specific details get blurred together; sliding-window attention keeps sharp memories but only within a fixed nearby span, so anything older simply falls out of range. Raven's routing lets the model choose, token by token, which slots to touch and how long each persists, so an old fact can survive undiluted. Separating where information gets written from how long it's allowed to persist lets a model keep old details sharp instead of blurring or discarding them.

Why it matters

Long-running AI agents need to recall specific facts from far back in a conversation without the cost of full attention over the whole history; an architecture that keeps recall high at linear, not quadratic, cost is a direct answer to that tradeoff.

Lidar finds 20,000 hidden earthworks under the Amazon canopy

What it is

A Nature paper published July 29 reports that airborne lidar — laser pulses that penetrate forest canopy to map the ground beneath — surveyed 4,430 km of flight lines over southwest Amazonia and found nearly 400 previously undocumented earthworks: ditches, geoglyphs, ponds, and wells, some over 1,500 years old. Extrapolating that density across similar unsurveyed terrain, the authors estimate more than 20,000 such earthworks remain hidden, built by populations they calculate could have reached roughly 3 million people across just 3% of the forest.

The principle

A forest canopy blocks ordinary aerial photography from ever seeing the ground, so for decades the absence of visible ruins was mistaken for the absence of people; lidar's pulses reach the ground through gaps in the leaves regardless of what's growing above, turning "we can't see anything" into "there was never anything to see" only because the tool, not the history, was missing. A forest hiding its ground from cameras isn't the same as a forest with nothing on the ground to hide.

Why it matters

It overturns the long-standing assumption that much of the Amazon was sparsely inhabited wilderness before European contact, suggesting instead a large, still largely undiscovered population that actively shaped the forest — with direct implications for how "untouched" rainforest conservation policy should treat this land.

New calculations resolve one muon mystery by breaking another

What it is

Newer lattice QCD calculations — a first-principles, supercomputer-based way of computing the strong nuclear force's contribution to the muon's magnetic moment — have moved into agreement with the actual measured value from Fermilab's muon g-2 experiment. That undercuts the older method, which computed the same quantity indirectly from electron-positron collision data and had been the basis for years of excitement that the muon anomaly pointed to physics beyond the Standard Model.

The principle

Physicists have long used two independent routes to estimate the same hard-to-compute piece of the muon's magnetic moment — one built from collision data fed through a dispersion relation, the other computed directly from the underlying quark-gluon theory on a supercomputer lattice. When the two disagreed, only one could be right, and it now looks like the lattice route is the one experiment favors. Two independent ways of computing the same quantity landed on different numbers, and only now is it becoming clear which one was ever measuring reality.

Why it matters

It reshapes one of the most closely watched anomalies in particle physics — a mismatch read for years as a hint of undiscovered particles — into what may turn out to be an artifact of the older calculation method rather than a signal of new physics.

A newly named organelle explains why some cows burp more methane

What it is

Researchers studying microbes in cow rumens identified and named "hydrogenobodies," hydrogen-producing organelles inside isotrich ciliates — one of two protozoan groups competing for space in a cow's gut. Measuring gas directly from 100 dairy cows with a head box, they found cows whose rumen microbiome was dominated by isotrichs released substantially more methane than cows dominated by the other major group, entodiniomorphs.

The principle

Methane in a cow's burps isn't made by the cow directly — it's made by hydrogen-consuming archaea living in the same gut community, and those archaea can only make as much methane as they have hydrogen to work with. Hydrogenobodies generate that hydrogen supply in the first place, so whichever protozoan group dominates a given cow's gut sets a ceiling on how much fuel is available downstream. The methane a cow burps is capped not by the cow but by how much hydrogen its gut microbes happen to be producing.

Why it matters

Livestock methane is a major driver of near-term warming, and if microbiome composition sets a cow's emissions ceiling, shifting that balance — via feed, additives, or breeding for gut-microbiome traits — becomes a concrete lever for cutting agricultural methane rather than an abstract one.

Magnitude 7.1 quake kills 25 in southern Japan

What it is

A magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck Kyushu, Japan's southern main island, on July 28, epicentered near the coastal city of Uto in Kumamoto prefecture. It triggered an explosion inside a shopping mall, toppled a factory chimney, and flattened homes; the confirmed death toll had climbed to at least 25 by July 30, with rescue crews still searching collapsed structures for survivors.

The principle

An earthquake's death toll comes less from the shaking itself than from what the shaking hits — a gas line under a mall, an aging chimney, houses built before modern codes — so the same magnitude quake produces wildly different outcomes depending on what kind of built environment happens to sit on top of the fault. The same shaking can be survivable or lethal depending entirely on what was already standing on top of it.

Why it matters

It's a fast-moving disaster with a rising toll and an ongoing search-and-rescue effort in a region not traditionally seen as Japan's highest earthquake risk zone, a reminder that damaging quakes aren't confined to the country's most famous fault lines.

Iran and US trade strikes again, ending a brief pause

What it is

Iran fired missiles at US forces stationed in Jordan overnight into July 29, which Jordan's military said it intercepted; the US and Saudi Arabia responded with joint strikes on Iran-aligned militia targets across seven Iraqi provinces, killing at least 20 fighters and several Iranian Revolutionary Guard advisers. The exchange ends the two-day mutual pause both sides had held on July 26–27, and Trump has since promised to hit Iran "hard" in response.

The principle

A pause that was never formalized as a ceasefire has nothing binding either side to it beyond ongoing convenience, so it lasts only as long as neither side sees an opening or a grievance worth acting on — which is exactly why it can end as abruptly as it began, with no agreement to violate and no negotiation to collapse. An informal pause has no floor under it — it can end the instant either side decides acting is worth more than restraint.

Why it matters

It shows how fragile the de-escalation covered days ago actually was, and how a strike on a third country in retaliation for an attack on a fourth keeps widening the set of places this conflict can flare up next.

Fed holds rates steady as inflation hawks demand a hike

What it is

The US Federal Reserve held its benchmark interest rate steady at 3.50–3.75% on July 29 for a fifth consecutive meeting, even as three regional Fed presidents dissented and pushed for a quarter-point hike — an unusually large three-way split. Chair Kevin Warsh attributed elevated inflation partly to energy-price spikes tied to the Trump administration's war footing against Iran since March. Major US stock indices fell more than 1.5% on the news.

The principle

A central bank balances two risks it can't fully separate — raising rates to fight inflation risks slowing an already-cooling job market, while holding steady risks letting a supply-driven price spike become embedded in expectations; when policymakers disagree sharply about which risk is bigger, that disagreement becomes visible as a rare multi-way dissent rather than a routine vote. A three-way dissent is the committee admitting in public that it can no longer agree which risk is the bigger one.

Why it matters

It signals a war-driven energy shock is now feeding directly into core US monetary policy, and the unusually sharp internal split suggests further hikes are more likely than markets had been pricing in if inflation stays elevated.
