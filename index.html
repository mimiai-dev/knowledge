Visual Studio's Copilot agent ships built-in .NET and Azure skills

What it is

GitHub's July update for Copilot in Visual Studio, shipped July 30, adds a new "Agent (Preview)" option built on the same GitHub Copilot SDK that powers the Copilot CLI, plus built-in skills authored by the .NET and Azure product teams that surface automatically in the tool picker once those workloads are installed.

The principle

Instead of retraining a model to be equally sharp at every language and platform, GitHub ships the agent runtime separately from swappable, expert-authored "skill" packages that inject domain knowledge on demand — so the same underlying agent gets better at .NET and Azure work without touching the model underneath. Separating the general agent runtime from swappable, expert-authored skill packages lets narrow expertise compound without retraining the model underneath it.

Why it matters

It's a concrete instance of the "agent skills" pattern spreading from chatbots into IDEs — specialized product teams package their own tacit expertise as instructions an agent can load on demand, rather than that expertise sitting only in docs a developer has to go find.

Copilot code review can now pull in your team's own tools

What it is

GitHub made two Copilot code review features generally available on July 29: agent skills, which let a review load a repo's own SKILL.md instructions and internal conventions, and MCP server connections, which let a review pull read-only context from external systems like issue trackers or documentation platforms, with each comment now flagged when it used one.

The principle

A review is only as good as what the reviewer knows about a change's context — house conventions, related tickets, prior incidents — and most of that has always lived outside the diff, in systems a human reviewer checks manually; MCP gives the review agent a standard way to reach into those systems directly, instead of reasoning from the diff alone. A code review agent limited to the diff can only catch what the diff itself reveals — everything else has to be handed to it explicitly.

Why it matters

Automated review tools have mostly competed on how well they read code; this shifts the competition toward how much of an organization's institutional context they can safely pull in, which is a much larger and more defensible surface to build on.

New benchmark turns real merged pull requests into agent tests

What it is

A paper posted July 31 introduces Change2Task, a pipeline that takes real merged pull requests from a repository's own history and converts them into runnable evaluation tasks: it reconstructs the codebase state the PR was originally written against, then verifies that both solving the reconstructed task and reapplying the original patch lead back to a working, restorable repository.

The principle

Hand-built coding-agent benchmarks tend to get memorized as models train on public code, but grounding tasks in a repository's own commit history instead lets fresh evaluation data be minted continuously from ordinary developer activity, and validating the full reconstruct-solve-restore cycle catches subtly broken tasks a hand-curated benchmark might miss. A benchmark that regenerates itself from a project's own ongoing history is harder to memorize than one built once and left standing.

Why it matters

Coding-agent evaluation has been bottlenecked by expensive, hand-curated environments; a scalable way to mint verified tasks straight from real repository activity addresses that bottleneck directly and could make benchmarks track real-world software work more closely.

Polishing a crystal's surface unlocks record quantum memory

What it is

A paper published in Nature Physics on July 30 traces phonon decoherence in quartz mechanical oscillators to the crystal's surface — lattice distortion, subsurface damage, and impurities left by ordinary polishing — rather than to the bulk material. Using laser spectroscopy to pin this down, the team applied an optimized chemical-mechanical polishing process to strip the damaged layer and built resonators with quality factors above 240 million at 12 GHz, a record for this class of device.

The principle

A quantum system decoheres wherever it's most exposed to uncontrolled interactions with its environment, and for a solid mechanical oscillator that exposure concentrates disproportionately at its outer surface rather than spreading through the bulk — so the fix isn't a better material, it's a better surface. Removing a few damaged atomic layers can matter more for a device's quantum coherence than the bulk material underneath it.

Why it matters

Long-lived, high-frequency mechanical oscillators are a candidate hardware platform for storing quantum information between operations; a manufacturing fix, not a new material, that pushes coherence and Q-factor to record levels can move directly into real quantum-computing and quantum-networking hardware.

Squeezing a crystal the right way can make it left- or right-handed

What it is

A paper published in Nature on July 29 reports the "piezochiral effect": mechanical strain applied to certain achiral crystals — ones identical to their own mirror image — makes them chiral, with a handedness that flips just by switching between stretching and compressing the crystal, or by changing the strain's direction. The team confirmed the effect in AgGaS2 by measuring how strained samples rotate polarized light.

The principle

Some achiral crystals are built from repeating units that each contain both a left-handed and a right-handed fragment locked together in balance; uniaxial strain distorts those two fragments unevenly, tipping the balance toward one handedness and making the whole crystal chiral without adding or removing a single atom. Chirality doesn't have to be built into a material's atoms — it can be summoned by asymmetrically deforming a shape that already contains both mirror images at once.

Why it matters

Chirality governs how a material interacts with circularly polarized light, spin-polarized electrons, and other chiral molecules, so a purely mechanical, reversible switch — instead of needing a different, permanently chiral material for each job — opens a new, tunable control knob for photonics, spintronics, and chiral sensing.

Bacteria's antiviral immune system turns out to work like ours

What it is

A paper published in Nature on July 29 systematically surveys STAND NTPases, a family of bacterial antiviral defense proteins, and finds at least 90 structurally distinct families that together recognize most of the core structural and replication proteins bacteriophages need to infect a cell. One previously uncharacterized family, Avs7, was shown by cryo-EM to assemble into a butterfly-shaped structure that detects a phage's capsid protein, incorporating a hijacked bacterial protein as part of its own sensing machinery.

The principle

STAND NTPases are structurally related to the NLR immune receptors plants and animals use to detect pathogens inside their own cells, so finding this many bacterial versions, each tuned to a different conserved viral part, shows the same sensing strategy — recognize an essential, hard-to-mutate pathogen component, then trigger a defensive response — evolved to defend single cells long before it was repurposed for plant and animal immunity. A pathogen-sensing architecture built to recognize the parts of an invader it can't afford to change shows up on both sides of the prokaryote-eukaryote divide.

Why it matters

Phage therapy is being revived as an antibiotic alternative, and a fuller map of how bacteria naturally detect and resist phages both explains why some phage therapies fail and supplies a toolkit of natural sensors that could be engineered into new biotechnology.

Trump announces Hamas disarmament deal, unconfirmed by either side

What it is

On July 30–31, President Trump announced that Hamas and other Gaza factions agreed to a phased disarmament plan brokered through his "Board of Peace": police-held weapons handed over first, then heavier weapons, with Israeli forces withdrawing in step and an international force plus a new Palestinian police taking over security. Neither Hamas nor Israel had confirmed the agreement at announcement, and Hamas said it would not proceed unless Israel meets its own obligations first.

The principle

An announced agreement and a binding one are different things whenever the announcement comes from a mediator rather than the parties themselves — the real test isn't the press statement, it's whether the two sides who actually have something to lose start visibly acting on its terms. A deal announced by the broker isn't a deal until the two sides who have to live with it start acting like it's real.

Why it matters

It's presented as the mechanism that unlocks a full Israeli withdrawal from Gaza, so whether Hamas and Israel actually follow through determines whether the broader ceasefire framework holds or collapses back into fighting.

Russian missile crashes in Poland, NATO scrambles jets

What it is

Early on July 30, a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile crossed into Polish airspace during a large overnight barrage on Ukraine that killed at least 8 civilians and wounded dozens; NATO forces in Poland scrambled fighter jets, an attack helicopter, and other aircraft and activated ground air defenses. The missile came down in a farming area in Lublin province; Polish officials said there was no indication Poland itself was the target.

The principle

NATO's collective-defense posture responds to what actually crosses its border, not to intent, so a missile entering Polish airspace forces the alliance to react to the physical fact of the intrusion regardless of whether Russia meant to hit Ukraine or Poland — that ambiguity is exactly what makes these incidents dangerous. An alliance built to respond to what actually crosses its border has no clean way to distinguish a stray weapon from a deliberate one in the moment it happens.

Why it matters

It's the latest in a recurring pattern of Russian weapons entering NATO airspace during strikes on Ukraine, and each repetition raises the odds that one lands somewhere, or on someone, that forces a response neither side intended to trigger.

Tens of thousands cross into Spain's Ceuta enclave, dozens die

What it is

Beginning July 30, tens of thousands of migrants crossed from Morocco into Ceuta, Spain's North African enclave, after rumors spread that a recent Spanish Supreme Court ruling meant people arriving by sea could no longer be immediately returned; at least 18–34 people died attempting the roughly 3–5 km swim. Spain deployed military units alongside police, and its interior minister said 25,000 of those who crossed had voluntarily returned to Morocco.

The principle

A legal ruling narrow enough to apply only to specific procedural circumstances can still trigger a mass-movement event once it's stripped of that nuance and passed along as a rumor — the crowd responds to what it's been told the ruling means, not to the ruling itself, and by the time authorities can correct the record the movement is already underway. A rumor about what a court ruling means can move tens of thousands of people faster than the actual ruling ever could.

Why it matters

It's one of the largest single-enclave migration surges Spain has faced, testing both its capacity to manage a sudden mass crossing and the EU's broader asylum framework, while exposing how quickly misinformation about legal procedure can turn into a humanitarian emergency.
